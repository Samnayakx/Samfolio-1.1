    Work/Gap 3D Fit

    Case study ( 04 ) · Gap

    Turning sizing uncertainty into a 61% add-to-bag decision.

    Gap Inc. acquired Drapr for its 3D fit. When the web launch moved up and testers ran out, I argued against waiting, rebuilt the flow from live session data and shipped on time. An A/B test showed +61% add-to-bag.

    Role
    Technical Product Designer
    Timeline
    6 months
    Owned
    Flow, fit logic, 3D performance budget · Web, iOS
    Team
    Me, 2 PMs, 1 UXR

    01Final Designs

    Ask little, then prove the size.

    Each step either sharpens the avatar or lets the shopper verify the size.

    What testers said

    • “Seeing the inches next to each control made me trust it more than guessing by eye.”

      ParticipantTested: numbers

    • “I opened 'Why this recommendation' out of curiosity. The size felt less random after.”

      ParticipantTested: reasons

    • “The ? told me what 'fitted' and 'loose' actually feel like.”

      ParticipantTested: fit words

    02Overview

    What if a 3D avatar could make shoppers trust a size, not only see it?

    Gap Inc. acquired Drapr, a Y Combinator 3D-fit startup, in 2021 and called fit “the number one point of friction for customers.” I led the 3D Fit redesign over six months, with two PMs and a UX researcher.

    • Problem

      The avatar showed fit. Shoppers still didn't believe the size.

    • The bet

      Less 3D realism, more reasons.

    • Result

      +61% add-to-bag, shipped on the earlier date.

    Initial ObservationsThe old flow showed fit. Shoppers still felt risk.

    Denim made sizing harder: one size had to explain multiple body zones without turning checkout into a long form.

    Working hypothesis

    A 3D avatar shows fit, but shoppers buy on trust.

    03What Existed Today

    Charts, diagrams and recommendations gave numbers, not confidence.

    04Understanding Users

    Online denim shoppers who needed a reason to believe a size.

    In month one I tested with 50 online denim shoppers, using our UX researcher's methods. Once recruiting stopped, I read live sessions in Microsoft Clarity.

    In their words

    • “A chart gives me a number. I still can't tell if the jeans will fit me.”

      ParticipantNeed: credible sizing

    • “If it just says size 8, I'm guessing. Tell me why it picked 8.”

      ParticipantNeed: evidence

    • “Ten questions and I'm gone. I'll order the size I always wear.”

      ParticipantNeed: little effort

    05Design Process

    The web launch moved up, and there were no testers left.

    Fifty tested users were all we had, and the new date left no time to recruit more. Management proposed holding the launch. I argued we could prove the design another way: sessions showed shoppers dropping off as the inputs piled up, before they saw any value.

    The bet

    Live session data could stand in for the testers we did not have.

    Design decisionsFour layouts, one 3D viewer, and why each lost.

    I held the 3D viewer constant and changed only how the flow asks for input. Each layout is scored against the three limits. Figures for the alternatives are illustrative; the 61% lift is measured.

    06Outcomes and Reflection

    An A/B test showed a 61% lift in add-to-bag.

    Shipped on the earlier date. The lift came from less sizing ambiguity, not more 3D realism.

    • Add-to-bag: +61% (A/B test)

      New flow against the old flow

    ReflectionI changed my mind about the job of 3D.

    The visual was not the product. Confidence was.

    When testers run out, the product already holds the evidence.
